# Executive readout: speaker notes

Three slides, about five minutes. The slides are in [`readout.pdf`](readout.pdf); these are the talking points and backup answers for each.

## Slide 1 · The answer and three decisions

**Headline:** Contact rose after our events, mostly in line with the season; we cannot yet show they build pipeline.

About 1.5 minutes. Lead with the answer: more attending firms were met after the events, but checked against a comparison group over the same dates, most of that rise is the normal mid-year fundraising season. London is the one exception. Nothing in the data shows the events created pipeline, and today we could not show it either way. Walk the four numbers left to right: spend, reach, contact up from 43% to 60% of attending firms (comparison firms went from 26% to 41% over the same dates, so about what the season gives), and the warning that one unverified $650M deal is 55% of all committed capital, so any fundraising total should be quoted with and without it. Then the three decisions, in priority order. Decision 2 is a service standard for our top-priority investors: follow-up is slow, but this data does not prove faster follow-up wins deals, so we test it. Decision 3: Berlin is the clearest event finding. It cost the most and we met fewer of its attendees after it than before, at every window we checked. London had the best follow-up at the lowest cost, but that rests on one dinner and a small comparison group, so it is a format to test, not a reason to move budget yet. The ask: agree these before the next event budget is set. If asked "so were the events worth it?": for contact, only London clearly added anything beyond the season; for pipeline, unproven rather than disproven, which is decision 1.

## Slide 2 · The evidence

**Headline:** Only London beat the season on contact, on a small sample. New pipeline did not rise.

About 2 minutes. Left side, what happened: more attending firms were met after events than before (43% to 60%). To check whether that is just the busy fundraising season, compare with firms that went to a different event and had no event of their own within 60 days, measured over the same dates. Across all three events, attending firms’ meetings rose 54% and the comparison group’s rose 62%: about the same, so overall the rise looks seasonal. London is the exception: attending firms went from 12 to 31 meetings, the comparison group from 6 to 8. Say the caveat out loud: that comparison is 9 firms; at a 30-day window those comparison firms picked up faster than London’s attendees, and at 90 days there are no comparison firms left. So London is a signal, not proof. Why the comparison group leaves some firms out: firms whose own event fell inside the window had their own follow-up meetings in the before period, which made them look flat and flattered our events; I found and removed that. Then Tier 1: 10 of 13 not met within 60 days of an event, 4 still not met by 90 days, so follow-up is slow. Right side, what it led to: attending firms opened new deals 6 points more often than before, firms at other events 11 points more. Deals opened after events did not close more often either: 13% versus 24% for deals already in progress, among deals old enough to have closed. How certain: resampling 5,000 times, both gaps have 95% ranges that include zero, so these are directional. Backup if asked. Is Berlin’s drop just overlap with London, 26 days earlier? No: Berlin attendees who did not go to London also fell, 11 to 6 meetings. Is New York’s rise real? The comparison firms went from 1 of 21 met to 7 of 21 over the same dates, about as much as New York’s attendees, so it looks seasonal. What about New York’s $225M of commitments from deals opened after the event? That is two deals, one of which skipped due diligence; it is an association, and across all events, deals opened after an event have not closed more often. At a 30-day window attending firms do open deals earlier (plus 10 vs minus 3 points), which fits events bringing deals forward rather than creating extra ones.

## Slide 3 · What to do this week and before the next event

**Headline:** Act on the follow-up gap now; measure the next event properly.

About 1.5 minutes. Left, this week: these four Tier 1 firms each have a live deal, three at IC, and no meeting logged since the event they attended. Together that is about $200M in target size. Ask: a named owner per firm, a CRM check, then a call. The CRM check matters: Firm 45’s deal reached IC with no meeting logged at all, so someone has been talking to them; across attending firms whose deals progressed, 4 of 37 have no meetings logged. Across all tiers the list is 14 firms, 12 with live deals worth $707M. Another 10 firms were met, just after 60 days, which is why the standard is speed. Be straight about the evidence: in this data, firms met within 30 days opened new deals at the same rate as firms met later (33% vs 33%), so the 30-day standard is a service level for our top investors that we test, not a proven driver of deals. Right, before the next event: four data changes so that next year we can answer the question this year’s data cannot, plus the format test (Berlin cost the most and contact fell after it; London did best on a small sample). Close with the $650M deal: it is 55% of committed capital and unverified, so check it before any fundraising total goes out. Backup if asked: three Tier 1 firms (two pension funds and an insurer) were never invited to any event; London beat the follow-up rate its investor mix would predict (72% vs 62%) and Berlin fell short (47% vs 59%), so the format finding is not just who attended.
