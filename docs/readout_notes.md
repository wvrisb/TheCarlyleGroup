# Executive readout: speaker notes

Three slides, about five minutes. The slides are in [`readout.pdf`](readout.pdf); these are the talking points and backup answers for each. The evidence and assumptions behind each recommendation are in [`assumptions.md`](assumptions.md).

## Slide 1 · The answer and three decisions

**Headline:** Meetings rose after our events, but nearly as much at firms that were not there. We cannot yet show that events bring in new deals.

After our events we met more of the firms that attended: 43% had a meeting with us in the 60 days before, 60% in the 60 days after. But May and June are busy months for meetings anyway, so I checked similar firms that were not at the event: firms we also invite, which went to a different event. Over the same weeks they rose almost as much, from 26% to 41%. So most of the rise would have happened without the event. London is the one exception. None of four checks shows a clear benefit from the events: meetings, new deals, deals that were already open, and whether deals closed. With three events, that means unproven, not disproven. Then the four numbers: what we spent, how many of our firms came, the rise in meetings, and the warning that one $650M deal we have not verified is 55% of all committed capital, so any fundraising total should be quoted with and without it. Then the three decisions. Decision 1: do not cut events on this evidence and do not expand them either; run the next events as a proper test, and use that test to try giving family offices more places, redesigning Berlin, and two more dinners. Decision 2: 34 deals worth $1.4B are recorded at the investor’s investment committee, the last step before committing, but of the 39 deals that reached that step only 2 committed. Either they are stuck, or the records are out of date; deal owners should confirm each one. Decision 3: 12 firms with open deals worth $707M have had no logged meeting since their event. Check the client database, then call. The ask: agree these three before the next event budget is set.

Backup if asked what the $1.2M got us: after the events, 30 new deals were opened with attending firms and 3 of them have committed $250M so far. But firms that went to a different event opened new deals just as often over the same weeks, so we cannot credit the events with those deals. That is the reason for running the next events as a test.

## Slide 2 · The evidence

**Headline:** Firms that were not at our events saw almost the same rise in meetings; only London stood out. Events did not bring more new deals than usual.

Left side, what happened: More of the firms that attended were met after events than before. May and June are busy months for meetings anyway, so to check whether the event made the difference, I compare with similar firms that were not at that event: firms we also invite, which went to a different one. I count their meetings over the same weeks. Across all three events, meetings with attending firms rose 54%, and meetings with those other firms rose 62%. About the same, so most of the rise would have happened without the event. London is the exception: meetings with its attendees went from 12 to 31, while the other firms went from 6 to 8. Say the caveat out loud: that comparison is only 9 firms, and it holds at 60 days but not at 30, so London is a signal, not proof. Then Tier 1: 10 of 13 had not been met 60 days after an event, and 4 still had not been met by 90 days. Follow-up happens, but slowly.

Right side, what it led to: The share of attending firms opening a new deal rose 6 points after events; for firms that went to a different event, 12 points over the same weeks. Deals that were already open moved forward no more often either, shown at the bottom right of the slide: 50% for attending firms against 55% for firms not at the event. And deals opened after events did not close more often: 13% against 19%, comparing deals of the same age, 4 to 8 months old, because older deals have had longer to close. How sure: with only three events, these differences are within the margin of error, so read them as direction, not proof. Backup if asked. Is Berlin’s drop just because many of its firms had been at London 26 days earlier? No: Berlin firms that skipped London also had fewer meetings, 11 down to 6. Part of it is our follow-up: in the 60 days after Berlin, only 11 of the team’s 31 meetings were with Berlin attendees and 15 were with London attendees. Is New York’s rise real? The other firms rose about as much over the same dates, so most of it would have happened anyway. New York’s $225M of commitments from deals opened after the event is two deals, one of which skipped due diligence; it is an association, not proof.

## Slide 3 · What to do this week and before the next events

**Headline:** This week: confirm the deals recorded at investment committee and call the firms we missed. Before the next events: set them up as a test.

Left, this week: First, the late-stage pipeline. 34 deals worth $1.4B are recorded at the investor’s investment committee, which is the last step before a commitment. But of the 39 deals that ever reached that step, only 2 committed and 3 were declined. Two explanations fit, and the data cannot tell them apart: the deals are stuck, or the records are out of date. The records point to the second: 9 of 14 commitments skipped the due diligence stage, and some firms’ deals moved forward with no meeting logged at all. Either way, ask each deal owner to confirm where the deal really is, close out the ones that are no longer live, and verify the $650M deal before any fundraising total goes out. Then the calls. These four Tier 1 firms each have an open deal, three of them at investment committee, and we have not logged a meeting with any of them since their event; together about $200M. Across all tiers, 12 firms with open deals worth $707M are in the same position. Check the client database first, because some meetings are not logged. Be straight about the evidence: firms met within 30 days opened new deals at the same rate as firms met later, 33% each, so this is good service for our most important investors, not a proven way to win deals.

Right, before the next events: Keep spending flat and run them as a test: record which event each deal came from, log meetings for every invited firm, compare with similar firms that were left out only because places ran out, and follow attending firms for 6 to 12 months. Use the test to try three changes. First, family offices: 6 of 14 of their deals that are at least four months old have committed, against 6 of 57 for every other type of investor, and the gap holds without the fast commitments that skipped due diligence. It is the clearest pattern in the data, but it comes from one year and from comparing many groups, so test it rather than assume it. Second, Berlin: it cost the most, $610K, and we met fewer of its firms afterwards, partly because our follow-up went to London attendees. Third, two more dinners, because London did best but on a small sample. Backup if asked: three Tier 1 firms were never invited to any event; and the $650M deal is from a foundation, so if it is real, foundations would be the biggest committers this year.
