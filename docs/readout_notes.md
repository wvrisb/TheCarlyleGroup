# Executive readout: speaker notes

Three slides, about five minutes. The slides are in [`readout.pdf`](readout.pdf); these are the talking points and backup answers for each.

## Slide 1 · The answer and three decisions

**Headline:** Meetings rose after our events, but nearly as much at firms that were not there. We cannot yet show that events bring in new deals.

About 1.5 minutes. Start with the answer. After our events we met more of the firms that attended: 43% had a meeting with us in the 60 days before, 60% in the 60 days after. But May and June are busy months for meetings anyway, so I checked similar firms that were not at the event: firms we also invite, which went to a different event. Over the same weeks they rose almost as much, from 26% to 41%. So most of the rise would have happened without the event. London is the one exception. On new deals, the data does not show the events made a difference, and today it could not show it either way, because nothing links a deal to the event behind it. Then the four numbers: what we spent, how many of our firms came, the rise in meetings, and the warning that one $650M deal we have not verified is 55% of all committed capital, so any fundraising total should be quoted with and without it. Then the three decisions, most important first. Decision 1 is about measurement: without it we will be in the same position next year. Decision 2: our follow-up with Tier 1 firms is slow. To be straight, this data does not prove faster follow-up wins deals, so we set the standard and test it. Decision 3: Berlin is the clearest event finding. It cost the most, and we met fewer of its firms after it than before, at every time frame we checked. Part of that is us: in the 60 days after Berlin, only 11 of the team’s 31 meetings were with Berlin attendees, and 15 were with London attendees from the dinner 26 days earlier. London did best for the lowest cost, but that is one dinner compared with 9 firms, so test the format before moving budget. The ask: agree these three before the next event budget is set. If asked whether the events were worth it: on meetings, only London clearly added more than would have happened anyway; on deals, we do not know yet, which is why decision 1 comes first.

## Slide 2 · The evidence

**Headline:** Firms that were not at our events saw almost the same rise in meetings; only London stood out. Events did not bring more new deals than usual.

About 2 minutes. Left side, what happened. More of the firms that attended were met after events than before. May and June are busy months for meetings anyway, so to check whether the event made the difference, I compare with similar firms that were not at that event: firms we also invite, which went to a different one. I count their meetings over the same weeks. Across all three events, meetings with attending firms rose 54%, and meetings with those other firms rose 62%. About the same, so most of the rise would have happened without the event. London is the exception: meetings with its attendees went from 12 to 31, while the other firms went from 6 to 8. Say the caveat out loud: that comparison is only 9 firms, and it holds at 60 days but not at 30, so London is a signal, not proof. If asked how the comparison was built: I left out firms that had their own event in the same period, because their own follow-up would have distorted the count and made our events look better. Then Tier 1: 10 of 13 had not been met 60 days after an event, and 4 still had not been met by 90 days. Follow-up happens, but slowly. Right side, what it led to. Attending firms opened new deals 6 points more often than before; firms at other events, 12 points more. So no extra new deals from the events. Deals opened after events also did not close more often: 13% against 19%, comparing deals of the same age, 4 to 8 months old, because older deals have had longer to close. How sure: with only three events, these differences are within the margin of error, so read them as direction, not proof. Backup if asked. Is Berlin’s drop just because many of its firms had been at London 26 days earlier? No: Berlin firms that skipped London also had fewer meetings, 11 down to 6. Is New York’s rise real? The other firms rose about as much over the same dates, 1 of 21 met to 7 of 21, so most of it would have happened anyway. What about New York’s $225M of commitments from deals opened after the event? That is two deals, one of which skipped due diligence. It is an association, and across all events deals opened after an event did not close more often. At 30 days attending firms do open deals a little earlier, which suggests events bring some deals forward rather than creating new ones.

## Slide 3 · What to do this week and before the next event

**Headline:** Call the firms we missed now, and set up the next event so we can measure it.

About 1.5 minutes. Left, this week. These four Tier 1 firms each have an open deal, three of them at investment committee, and we have not logged a meeting with any of them since their event. Together that is about $200M in deal size. The ask: a named owner for each firm, a quick CRM check, then a call. The CRM check matters: Firm 45’s deal reached investment committee with no meeting logged at all, so someone has been talking to them and not recording it; 4 attending firms are like this. Across all tiers the list is 14 firms, 12 with open deals worth $707M. Another 10 firms were met, just more than 60 days later, which is why the standard is about speed. Be straight about the evidence: firms met within 30 days opened new deals at the same rate as firms met later, 33% each, so the 30-day standard is a service level for our most important investors that we test, not a proven way to win deals. Right, before the next event: four changes to what we record, so next year we can answer the question this year’s data cannot, plus the format test. Berlin cost the most and we met fewer of its firms afterwards; London did best, on a small sample. Close with the $650M deal: it is 55% of committed capital and not verified, so check it before any fundraising total goes out. Backup if asked. Three Tier 1 firms, two pension funds and an insurer, were never invited to any event. London’s follow-up beat what its mix of investor types would predict, 72% against 62%, and Berlin fell short, 47% against 59%, so the format finding is not just about who attended. Was Berlin itself the problem? Partly it was our follow-up: in the 60 days after Berlin, only 11 of the team’s 31 meetings were with Berlin attendees and 15 were with London attendees. When two events are close together, name the follow-up owners for the second one before the first one’s follow-up is done.
